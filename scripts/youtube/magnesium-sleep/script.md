| 1 | Magnesium for sleep | Can't sleep, and someone told you to try magnesium? Here is what it can do, what it can't, and how to take it safely. |  |
| 2 | The short answer | The short answer. Magnesium may help a little, mostly if you are not getting enough of it. It is not a sleeping pill. |  |
| 3 | What magnesium is | Magnesium is a mineral your nerves and muscles depend on. Adults need roughly 300 to 420 milligrams a day, and about half of Americans get less than that from food. | S1 |
| 4 | Why a blood test won't tell you | You cannot easily test for it either. Less than 1% of the magnesium in your body is in your blood, so a standard blood test does not show how much you really have. | S1 |
| 5 | Who is more likely to run low | Some people are more likely to run low. Older adults, people with type 2 diabetes, people with gut conditions such as Crohn's or coeliac disease, and people who drink heavily. Long-term use of acid-reducing medicines called proton pump inhibitors can lower it too. | S1 |
| 6 | Why it might help | Why might it help? Magnesium helps regulate the signals that calm the nervous system. That explains the idea. It does not prove a supplement will improve your sleep. | U1 |
| 7 | What the older trials show | So what do trials show? A review of three trials in older adults with insomnia found people fell asleep about 17 minutes faster on magnesium. But total sleep time did not clearly improve, the trials were small, and the evidence was rated low to very low quality. | S2 |
| 8 | The 2025 trial | A newer trial gave 155 adults 250 milligrams of magnesium bisglycinate a day. After four weeks, their insomnia scores improved slightly more than with placebo. The effect was small. | S3 |
| 9 | Who benefits most | The people who improved most were the ones eating the least magnesium. That is an early signal, but it fits: it is most likely to help if you are running low. | S3 |
| 10 | Which form | Which form? Ones that dissolve well, like citrate, are absorbed better than oxide. Glycinate is the form that newer trial used. Oxide, carbonate, chloride and gluconate are the ones most likely to cause diarrhoea. | S1, S3 |
| 11 | 'Special' sleep forms | You will also see special forms sold for sleep. No form has been shown to beat the others for sleep in head-to-head trials. | U2 |
| 12 | How much | How much? For adults, the upper limit for magnesium from supplements is 350 milligrams a day. Check the label for elemental magnesium, not the weight of the whole compound. | S1 |
| 13 | Side effects | Too much causes diarrhoea, nausea and stomach cramps. If that happens, lower the dose or change the form. | S1 |
| 14 | Why the gut reacts | That is no accident. Magnesium is the active ingredient in some laxatives. One tablespoon of milk of magnesia contains 500 milligrams, well over the supplement limit. | S1 |
| 15 | Food first | Food counts too, and magnesium from food has no upper limit. An ounce of pumpkin seeds has about 156 milligrams. An ounce of almonds, 80. Half a cup of cooked spinach, 78. Black beans, brown rice and yogurt all add more. | S1 |
| 16 | Whole grains and absorption | Refining grains strips out much of the magnesium, so whole grains beat white bread and white rice. And your body absorbs only 30 to 40% of what you eat. | S1 |
| 17 | Who should check first | Who should check first? Anyone with kidney disease, because the kidneys clear extra magnesium. It can also stop some antibiotics and osteoporosis medicines being absorbed, so take them at least two hours apart and ask a pharmacist. Some water tablets change magnesium levels as well. | S1 |
| 18 | When to see a doctor | If you have slept badly for more than three months, see a doctor. Long-term insomnia has proven treatments, and a supplement should not delay them. | U3 |
| 19 | The basics | And the basics still matter more: a regular sleep window, less caffeine late in the day, and a dark, cool room. | U4 |
| 20 | The verdict | So. Magnesium is cheap, and worth a try if your diet is low in it. Expect a small effect, stay under 350 milligrams from supplements, and judge it after four weeks. Sources are in the description. This is general information, not medical advice. |  |

## Sources

- **S1.** NIH Office of Dietary Supplements, Magnesium fact sheet for health professionals: adult RDA 310 to 420 mg; 48% of Americans get less than recommended from food; 50 to 60% of body magnesium in bone, under 1% in blood serum, and serum levels do not reflect total body magnesium; groups at risk: older adults, type 2 diabetes, gastrointestinal diseases (Crohn's, coeliac), alcohol dependence; prolonged proton pump inhibitor use (typically more than a year) can cause low magnesium; upper limit 350 mg a day from supplements and medicines only; citrate absorbed better than oxide; carbonate, chloride, gluconate and oxide most often cause diarrhoea; milk of magnesia 500 mg elemental magnesium per tablespoon; food table (pumpkin seeds 156 mg per oz, almonds 80 mg per oz, boiled spinach 78 mg per half cup, black beans 60 mg per half cup, brown rice 42 mg per half cup, plain yogurt 42 mg per 8 oz); refining grains lowers magnesium content; 30 to 40% of dietary magnesium absorbed; kidney risk; bisphosphonates at least 2 hours apart; tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics; loop and thiazide diuretics increase magnesium loss. https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Magnesium-HealthProfessional/
- **S2.** Mah and Pitre 2021, BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies: 3 randomised trials, 151 older adults; sleep onset 17.36 minutes shorter than placebo; total sleep time not significantly improved; evidence rated low to very low. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12906-021-03297-z
- **S3.** Schuster et al. 2025, Nature and Science of Sleep: 155 adults; 250 mg elemental magnesium as bisglycinate daily; Insomnia Severity Index fell 3.9 points versus 2.3 on placebo at week 4; small effect (Cohen's d = 0.2); exploratory signal of larger benefit with lower dietary magnesium intake. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40918053/
